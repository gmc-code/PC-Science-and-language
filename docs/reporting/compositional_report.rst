Plant Biology: The Main Parts of a Flowering Plant
======================================================

Flowering plants are complex living organisms that make up the majority of plant life on Earth. To survive, grow, and reproduce, a flowering plant relies on a body system made of several specialized structural parts. These parts are divided into two main systems: the underground root system and the above-ground shoot system. Each component has a distinct structure and performs a specific job to keep the whole plant healthy.

**The Underground Root System**

The lowest part of a flowering plant is the **root system**, which typically grows beneath the soil surface. Roots consist of a main central root with many smaller branching root hairs extending outward into the dirt. The primary function of the root system is to anchor the plant firmly in the ground so it is not blown away by wind. Additionally, roots absorb water and essential mineral nutrients from the soil, which are then transported upward to feed the rest of the plant.

**The Above-Ground Shoot System**

Above the soil lies the shoot system, which consists of the stem, leaves, and flowers. The **stem** acts as the main central support column for the plant. Its main function is to hold the leaves up toward the sunlight and transport water, food, and nutrients between the roots and the upper body through tiny internal tubes.

Attached to the stem are the **leaves**, which are flat, green structures designed to capture light. The leaves act as the plant's food factories by using sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide to produce glucose through photosynthesis.

Finally, the **flowers** are the reproductive organs of the plant. Flowers contain colourful petals to attract insects and specialized reproductive parts that produce seeds, allowing new plants to grow.

Atomic Physics: The Structural Parts of an Atom
======================================================

All matter in the universe, from the air we breathe to the water we drink, is made of tiny particles called atoms. An atom is the basic building block of all chemical elements. Although atoms are incredibly small, they are not solid spheres; instead, they are composed of three subatomic particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons. These parts are organized into two main regions: a heavy central core and an outer surrounding cloud.

**The Central Nucleus**

At the very centre of an atom lies the **nucleus**, an extremely dense core that contains almost all of the atom's total weight. The nucleus is built from two types of subatomic particles tightly bound together. **Protons** are positively charged particles located inside the nucleus, and the number of protons determines what kind of chemical element the atom is.

Sitting alongside the protons are **neutrons**, which are uncharged particles. Their main function is to act as a nuclear glue, adding mass and keeping the positively charged protons from pushing each other apart.

**The Electron Cloud**

Surrounding the central nucleus is the **electron cloud**, an expansive region of mostly empty space where tiny **electrons** orbit. The **electrons** are extremely light particles with a negative electrical charge.

Because opposite charges attract, the negatively charged electrons are pulled toward the positively charged nucleus, holding the atom together. The arrangement and movement of these outer electrons govern how atoms interact and bond with each other to form chemicals.

Chemical Solutions: Understanding Solutes and Solvents
======================================================

In chemistry, a solution is a special type of homogeneous mixture where two or more substances are evenly blended together at a microscopic level. Solutions are common in everyday life, ranging from saltwater in the ocean to sugar in drinks. Every liquid solution is composed of two primary parts: a dissolved substance called a solute and a liquid medium called a solvent.

**The Dissolved Substance: The Solute**

The **solute** is the component of a solution that is present in the smaller amount and dissolves during mixing. A solute can be a solid (such as salt or sugar), a liquid (such as lemon juice), or a gas (such as carbon dioxide in soft drinks).

The primary function of the solute is to distribute its particles evenly throughout the liquid. When mixed, solute particles break apart from one another and occupy the spaces between the surrounding liquid particles until they become invisible to the naked eye.

**The Dissolving Medium: The Solvent**

The **solvent** is the component of a solution that is present in the larger amount and does the work of dissolving. The most common example of a liquid solvent in nature is **water**.

Because water can dissolve so many different substances, scientists often refer to it as the universal solvent. The main function of the solvent is to pull the solute particles apart and hold them in a stable, uniform suspension so that the solution maintains the same properties throughout.

Geology: The Internal Layers of the Earth
======================================================

The Earth is a giant, spherical planet made of rock, metal, and soil. Rather than being a solid ball of uniform material, the Earth is composed of four distinct structural layers organized by density and temperature. From the surface where we live down to the planet's centre, these layers are the crust, the mantle, the outer core, and the inner core.

**The Surface Layer: The Crust**

The outermost layer of the Earth is the **crust**, a thin, cold shell of solid rock that wraps around the planet like the skin on an apple. The crust includes both continental landmasses and ocean floors. The primary function of the crust is to provide a stable, solid surface that supports soil, oceans, atmosphere, and living ecosystems.

**The Middle Region: The Mantle**

Directly beneath the crust sits the **mantle**, the thickest layer of the Earth, accounting for over eighty percent of the planet's total volume. The mantle is composed of hot, semi-solid silicate rock.

Due to intense heat from below, the mantle slowly flows in giant churning movements called convection currents. These slow movements carry the overlying crustal plates, driving plate tectonics, mountain formation, and volcanic activity on the surface.

**The Central Core: Outer and Inner Core**

At the very centre of the Earth lies the core, which is divided into two distinct metallic parts. The **outer core** is a thick layer mainly composed of liquid iron that spins rapidly as the Earth rotates, generating the magnetic field that shields our planet from solar radiation.

Deep inside, the **inner core** is a solid ball of iron and nickel. Despite reaching scorching temperatures as hot as the surface of the sun, the tremendous pressure from all the surrounding layers above squeezes the inner core so tightly that its metal atoms cannot melt into a liquid.
