Cellular Structures: Comparing Plant and Animal Cells
======================================================

All living things are made of tiny building blocks called cells. Scientists divide these cells into two main groups: plant cells and animal cells. Both cell types have many of the same parts to stay alive, but they also have key differences that help them do different jobs.

**Parts Found in Both Cells**

At a basic level, plant and animal cells share several core parts that keep them working properly. Both cell types are enclosed by a thin outer layer called a **cell membrane**, which acts like a protective skin by controlling what moves in and out of the cell. Inside this boundary is the **cytoplasm**, a clear, jelly-like liquid that fills the cell and holds all the internal parts in place. Suspended within the cytoplasm is the **nucleus**, which acts as the control centre by storing genetic material and telling the cell what to do. Finally, both plant and animal cells rely on **mitochondria**, which act as cellular powerhouses by breaking down food to release energy.

**Key Differences Between Plant and Animal Cells**

Even though they share these fundamental parts, plant cells have extra structures that animal cells do not have. The first big difference is the outer boundary of the cell. Plant cells are surrounded by a tough, rigid **cell wall** made of cellulose, which sits outside the cell membrane to give the plant support so it can stand upright. In contrast, animal cells do not have a cell wall, which allows them to change shape and move more easily.

A second difference is how the two cell types obtain their food. Plant cells contain green parts called **chloroplasts**, which trap sunlight to make food through a process called photosynthesis. Animal cells do not have chloroplasts, so animals must eat other living things to get their energy. Finally, the storage spaces inside the cells are different. A plant cell usually contains one very large **vacuole** filled with water to keep the cell firm, whereas animal cells only have a few very small vacuoles used for storing waste.

Earth Materials: Comparing Igneous and Sedimentary Rocks
=========================================================

The Earth's crust is made of solid materials called rocks. Geologists group rocks into different categories depending on how they are made. Two major types of rock found on Earth are igneous rocks and sedimentary rocks. Both types are made of natural minerals, but they form in very different ways and look distinct from one another.

**What Both Rock Types Share**

As common components of the Earth's crust, igneous and sedimentary rocks share a few important physical properties. Both rock types are made from natural **minerals** like quartz and feldspar, which give the rocks their solid structure and weight. Additionally, both types make up large parts of the land we walk on as well as the ocean floor beneath us. Over millions of years, forces like heat, wind, and rain can break down or melt either rock type as part of the continuous rock cycle.

**How the Two Rock Types Differ**

The main differences between igneous and sedimentary rocks come from how they were created in nature. **Igneous rocks** form when melted rock cools down and turns solid. Deep underground, this melted rock is called magma, while above ground it is called lava. As the magma or lava cools, minerals grow together into hard, interlocking crystals. Because igneous rocks form from extremely hot melted rock, they almost never contain fossils.

In contrast, **sedimentary rocks** form at the Earth's surface from tiny pieces of broken rock, sand, and mud called sediments. Over a long time, layers of sediment pile up on top of each other in oceans, rivers, and deserts. The heavy weight squashes the bottom layers, gluing the sediments together into solid rock. Because of this step-by-step process, sedimentary rocks often show clear horizontal layers, and they are the main type of rock that contains fossils.

Simple Machines: Comparing Force Multipliers and Speed Multipliers
=====================================================================

Simple machines are tools that make work easier by changing the direction or size of a force. One of the most common simple machines is a lever, which consists of a rigid bar that pivots around a fixed point called a fulcrum. Geologists and engineers classify levers based on how they move loads. Two main ways a lever can operate are as a force multiplier or as a speed multiplier. While both types of levers use an input effort to move a load, they are designed to achieve completely different physical advantages.

**Shared Features of Levers**

At a fundamental level, all levers share the same basic mechanical parts regardless of their purpose. Every lever relies on a **fulcrum**, which acts as the turning point for the bar. Every lever also requires an **effort force**, which is the push or pull applied by a person or motor, and a **load force**, which is the object or resistance being moved. In both force multipliers and speed multipliers, changing the distance between these forces and the fulcrum determines how the machine behaves.

**Key Differences in Distance and Mechanical Advantage**

The main difference between a force multiplier and a speed multiplier depends on where the effort and load are placed relative to the fulcrum. A **force multiplier lever** is designed to make heavy objects easier to move. It places the effort force further away from the fulcrum than the load force. Because the effort arm is longer, a small input force creates a much larger output force at the load end. Common examples include crowbars and nutcrackers. However, the trade-off is distance: the effort force must travel a long way just to move the load a small distance.

In contrast, a **speed multiplier lever** is designed to make objects move faster and further rather than making them feel lighter. It places the effort force closer to the fulcrum than the load force. Because the load arm is longer, a small, strong movement at the effort end causes the load end to move a much greater distance in the same amount of time. Tools like broomsticks, baseball bats, and fishing rods are speed multipliers. On the other hand, the trade-off here is force: the user must apply a much larger effort force to move a comparatively light load.

Chemical Substances: Comparing Acids and Bases
======================================================

All matter is made of chemical substances that behave in different ways when they react with other materials. Scientists classify many common household and laboratory chemicals into two major groups: acids and bases. While both acids and bases are reactive solutions that can be identified using indicators, they exhibit opposite chemical properties and react differently with everyday substances.

**Shared Chemical Properties**

At a fundamental level, acids and bases share several important chemical features. Both substances are often liquid solutions containing charged particles called ions, which allow them to conduct electricity. Additionally, both acids and bases can be corrosive, meaning strong versions of either chemical can burn skin or damage materials. Scientists measure how strong or weak both acids and bases are by using the **pH scale**, which runs from 0 to 14. Furthermore, when an acid and a base are mixed together in equal amounts, they undergo a **neutralisation reaction** to form water and a salt.

**Key Differences in Taste, Feel, and Reactivity**

Despite these shared properties, acids and bases have contrasting physical and chemical characteristics. An **acid** is a substance with a pH value lower than 7. Acids generally have a sour taste, which is why everyday foods like lemons, vinegar, and yoghurt contain mild acids. When tested with litmus paper—a common chemical indicator—acids turn blue litmus paper red. In chemical reactions, acids react with metals to produce hydrogen gas and can dissolve limestone rocks.

In contrast, a **base** is a substance with a pH value greater than 7. Bases have a bitter taste and feel slippery or soapy to touch, which makes them useful for cleaning products such as soaps, drain cleaners, and toothpaste. Unlike acids, bases turn red litmus paper blue and do not react with metals to produce gas. While acids are characterized by having an excess of hydrogen ions, bases are characterized by having an excess of hydroxide ions, giving them their opposite chemical behavior.
